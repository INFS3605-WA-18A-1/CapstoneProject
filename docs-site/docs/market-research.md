# Market Research

## Problem Statement V 1.0
> Currently the version of this document is from the seminar **check individual contribution for additional problem statements**.

The main problem facing individual property managers is the follow up and continual re-scheduling of contractors for fixing repair issues that have arisen for current tenants. This affects the current tenants and property owners as the former needs to live with the issues for a prolonged period of time and the property owner needs to pay additional funds to complete repairs.

This needs to change as it adds additional load to the property managers administrative processes and hinders the SMB from growth in management of additional properties.

<h3> Who are they?</h3>
According to the latest [ABS data](https://www.abs.gov.au/statistics/economy/business-indicators/counts-australian-businesses-including-entries-and-exits/jul2022-jun2026) (as at 30 June 2026), there were 2.8 million actively trading businesses in Australia, with 996,203 of them employing staff, which means roughly 1.82 million had no employees at all. Small businesses (fewer than 20 employees) make up 97% of all actively trading businesses, or about 2.7 million, so the great majority of small businesses are non-employing sole traders or partnerships. On employment, the most recent figures show small businesses employed over 5 million people, which is 39% of the private sector workforce in 2023-24 ([ASBFEO Small Business Data Portal](https://www.asbfeo.gov.au/small-business-data-portal/number-small-businesses-australia)).
**The standard definition for SMB is a compnay that hires 1-20 employees for "small" businesses and 21-100 for medium businesses. These businesses must also be earning revenue less than $50 million annually for small and less than $1 billion annually for medium sized businesses.**

<h3>Owners Time</h3>
A large share of the owner's week goes on **admin work** rather than on customers or growth. A global [Sage survey](https://sage-productivity.azurewebsites.net/assets/report-on-admin-burden.pdf) of **over 3,000 SMBs found the average Australian business spends 81 days per year on admin tasks** (vs a 120-day global average, [Sage](https://www.sage.com/en-au/blog/)), and one summary of [Xero data](https://www.xero.com/au/resources/small-business-insights/) puts it at 10.5 hours a week, with financial admin the heaviest part. **One in three owners say financial admin is their most difficult admin task, followed by marketing and sales (22%) and legal and compliance (20.3%), and chasing money is a big piece of it, since 44% of Australian small business invoices get paid late** ([Xero Small Business Insights](https://www.xero.com/au/resources/small-business-insights/)). As for decisions, the structure of the sector largely answers that: with about two-thirds of small businesses having no employees ([ABS](https://www.abs.gov.au/statistics/economy/business-indicators/counts-australian-businesses-including-entries-and-exits/jul2022-jun2026)) and most of the rest employing one to four people, the owner is usually the only decision-maker, so pricing, quoting, hiring, purchasing, cash flow and compliance all wait on one person (this is an inference from the business counts, not a surveyed figure). The cost shows up in what gets pushed aside: 42% of SMB leaders surveyed by [Dext](https://dext.com/au/news/half-of-australian-smes-fear-closure-yet-many-still-handle-finances-alone) said they had missed business opportunities due to time lost to financial admin, and owners in an older [Reckon survey](https://dynamicbusiness.com.au/featured/admin-killing-the-dream-of-australian-small-businesses.html) reported spending more time each week on administration and payroll (10 or more hours) than operating at their peak (fewer than 10 hours).

**Therefore the main goal for the designed product is to assist in growth despite the admin work requirement** This means either being able to distribute the owner's decision making to multipler areas (Stream 1), lessen the admin work requirement (Stream 2), or train the work force for AI adoption for a more efficient process (Stream 3).

<h3>SMB AI Usage</h3>
At the strict end, the [ABS found](https://www.abs.gov.au/media-centre/media-releases/business-adoption-artificial-intelligence-accelerates-2024-25) about 12 per cent of Australian businesses used AI in 2024–25, falling to around 11 per cent for small and micro businesses. Broader surveys come in much higher: [NAB research](https://www.nab.com.au/news/technology-ai/small-business-in-the-driver-s-seat-of-australia-s-ai-shift) puts active AI use at 42% of SMEs, with another 14% planning to adopt, the [National AI Centre's tracker](https://www.ai.gov.au/news-and-insights/blog/ai-adoption-insights-december-2025-february-2026) recorded 44% in February 2026, an [Intuit QuickBooks survey](https://quickbooks.intuit.com/au/blog/news/ai-impact-report-australia-2026/) found regular use reached 69% in January 2026, and auDA's *Digital Lives of Australians 2026* found 72% of small businesses had used AI ([via SEO Summer](https://www.seosummer.com/australian-business-ai-adoption-2026/)). The tools are mostly general-purpose assistants and features built into software they already use, ranging from free chatbots to agentic AI tools, applied to routine work: data processing (38%), administration (36%), marketing (34%), customer service (32%), and bookkeeping (32%) ([Intuit QuickBooks](https://quickbooks.intuit.com/au/blog/news/ai-impact-report-australia-2026/)). NAB respondents most often named automating admin tasks as the main benefit (64%), and [Xero data](https://www.xero.com/au/resources/small-business-insights/) (via [Melbourne AI Agents](https://www.melbourneaiagents.com.au/resources/ai-readiness-report-2026)) suggests a third of small businesses using AI save 11 to 20 staff hours a month. The evidence points more to stalling than quitting: [Waymouth Tech](https://www.waymouthtech.com/blog/ai-adoption-australian-smb-statistics) notes that most SMBs use off-the-shelf assistants for writing and summarising while far fewer have AI built into core workflows, only 12% of Intuit's respondents call AI core to their operations, and the [National AI Centre tracker](https://www.ai.gov.au/news-and-insights/reports/ai-adoption-tracker) dipped from 45% to 43% between quarters. Waymouth also lists open-ended chatbots, vague "AI strategy" projects and over-ambitious agent systems as the uses most likely to underperform. Both sources you supplied are AI consultancies, so they have a commercial interest in the topic; the [Melbourne AI Agents page](https://www.melbourneaiagents.com.au/resources/ai-readiness-report-2026) is mostly a roundup of NAB, [MYOB](https://www.myob.com/au/about/media-centre), Xero, [Deloitte](https://www.deloitte.com/au/en/about/press-room/ai-edge-small-business-increased-smb-ai-adoption-can-add-44-billion-australias-economy-251125.html) and ABS figures, and the Waymouth piece is largely commentary rather than new data.

The upside of closing the gap is large. [Deloitte Access Economics](https://www.deloitte.com/au/en/about/press-room/ai-edge-small-business-increased-smb-ai-adoption-can-add-44-billion-australias-economy-251125.html) models that wider SMB AI adoption could add $44 billion to the economy and lift profitability by 45–111%, [MYOB](https://www.myob.com/au/about/media-centre) data shows SMEs using AI products growing 2.8× faster than non-users, and the [Tech Council of Australia and Microsoft](https://techcouncil.com.au/newsroom/generative-ai-could-contribute-115-billion-annually-to-australias-economy-by-2030) estimate generative AI could contribute $115 billion a year by 2030.

<h3>AI Usage Blockers</h3>
The main blocker for AI usage depends on the market, and on whether the business has tried AI yet. The surveys split into two groups that report different problems:

| SMEs **not** using AI | "AI is not relevant to my business" | 54% | [National AI Centre, Dec 2025 – Feb 2026](https://www.ai.gov.au/news-and-insights/blog/ai-adoption-insights-december-2025-february-2026) |

| SMBs using or considering AI | Privacy and security | 39% (higher than US 36%, UK 35%) | [Intuit QuickBooks 2026 Impact Report](https://quickbooks.intuit.com/au/blog/news/ai-impact-report-australia-2026/) |

| Mid-sized businesses (20–500 staff) | AI tools not connected to other systems | only 23% connected | [Decidr, State of AI in Australian Business 2026](https://www.smallbizpromptshop.com.au/blog/new-data-australian-small-businesses-arent-behind-on-ai-tools-theyre-behind-on-everything-underneath-them) |

**What comes up most:** for businesses that haven't started, it is **trust and relevance** rather than cost. Two in three non-adopters would rather keep a human in control, and more than half don't see how AI applies to their work. For businesses already using AI, the blocker moves to **privacy and confidence** (privacy 39%, fear of getting it wrong 39%, not knowing what it can do 31%), the same three barriers [Accountants Daily](https://www.accountantsdaily.com.au/technology/22420-the-ai-confidence-gap-holding-australian-small-businesses-back) highlights. Cost comes up less than you'd expect. The [ABS barriers](https://www.abs.gov.au/statistics/industry/technology-and-innovation/characteristics-australian-business) that mention money (cost/benefit uncertainty 13%, lack of funds 15% for innovation) sit below skills and time; and when the ABS asked about barriers to collaboration, **insufficient time (26%)** was the top answer.

**Stalling vs stopping:** No reliable Australian figure was found for businesses that tried AI and then *stopped*. What the data does show is stalling at shallow use:
- Only 12% of [Intuit's respondents](https://quickbooks.intuit.com/au/blog/news/ai-impact-report-australia-2026/) call AI core to their operations, even though 69% use it regularly.
- [Employment Hero's SME Pulse](https://www.intelligentsme.tech/2026/09/22/why-small-australian-businesses-are-getting-left-behind-on-ai/) (Apr–Jun 2026, 600 leaders) found only 13% of micro businesses use AI company-wide; 37% describe their use as "limited", 22% are not using it in any meaningful way, and 25% have no AI investment at all. *(Employment Hero defines micro as fewer than 21 staff, which is different from the ABS definition.)*
- The [National AI Centre tracker](https://www.ai.gov.au/news-and-insights/blog/ai-adoption-insights-december-2025-february-2026) dipped from 45% to 43% between quarters.
- COSBOA has warned that 2.7 million small businesses need support to see a payoff from AI ([Small Business Connections](https://smallbusinessconnections.com.au/australian-small-businesses-are-using-ai-but-many-still-arent-seeing-the-payoff)).

**Blockers are different by sector.** The [tracker](https://www.ai.gov.au/news-and-insights/blog/ai-adoption-insights-december-2025-february-2026) splits sectors: construction and agriculture are below 30% adoption, while health, education and services are above 50%. An earlier quarter put construction at 34% adopting and 25% not aware of how to use AI ([National AI Centre, 2024 Q4](https://www.industry.gov.au/news/ai-adoption-australian-businesses-2024-q4)). The [ABS figures](https://www.abs.gov.au/statistics/industry/technology-and-innovation/characteristics-australian-business/latest-release) show a similar split (construction 6%, info media & telco 38%). This is why the next section looks at where the gap is largest.

## High Potential Markets

"High potential" here means **lots of businesses × low current AI use × a heavy admin load that AI is already good at** (quoting, invoicing, scheduling, bookkeeping). The [ABS Business Characteristics Survey](https://www.abs.gov.au/statistics/industry/technology-and-innovation/characteristics-australian-business/latest-release) gives the strictest AI-use figures by industry. Business counts are from [ABS Counts of Australian Businesses](https://www.abs.gov.au/statistics/economy/business-indicators/counts-australian-businesses-including-entries-and-exits/latest-release), with the construction count via [Money.com.au](https://www.money.com.au/business-loans/australian-business-statistics) (June 2025):

**The largest AI adoption gap in the market is construction trades.** Construction is Australia's biggest industry by business count ([Money.com.au](https://www.money.com.au/business-loans/australian-business-statistics)), yet only about 6% of construction businesses reported using AI (half the 12% national rate) ([ABS](https://www.abs.gov.au/statistics/industry/technology-and-innovation/characteristics-australian-business/latest-release)). The [government tracker](https://www.ai.gov.au/news-and-insights/blog/ai-adoption-insights-december-2025-february-2026) agrees, with construction under 30% against more than 50% in health and services. Agriculture and transport have lower rates, but they have far fewer businesses (170k and 261k compared with 463k). Construction also carries the most financial distress: it made up 27% of all companies entering external administration in 2023–24 (2,975 companies, [ASIC](https://www.asic.gov.au/about-asic/news-centre/news-items/annual-asic-insolvency-data-reveals-increase-in-companies-failing/)).

The sector is almost entirely small. About 98.5% of construction firms have fewer than 20 employees and 91% have fewer than 5 ([Builders Institute](https://bi.edu.au/news/small-construction-businesses-the-backbone-of-australias-building-industry-2025-insights/), citing ABS/CEDA, June 2024). Most of these are **construction services**, meaning the trades: plumbing, electrical, carpentry, tiling and plastering.

**However, the biggest opportunity area for AI is the agricultural sector.** Agriculture, forestry and fishing has 170,185 businesses. The number shrank 0.5% in 2025–26, while total business numbers grew ([ABS, Counts of Australian Businesses](https://www.abs.gov.au/statistics/economy/business-indicators/counts-australian-businesses-including-entries-and-exits/latest-release)). Only **3%** of agriculture businesses used AI in 2024–25, a quarter of the 12% national rate ([ABS, Characteristics of Australian Business 2024–25](https://www.abs.gov.au/statistics/industry/technology-and-innovation/characteristics-australian-business/latest-release)). Even on the National AI Centre's broader measure, agriculture sits below 30%, compared with over 50% in health and services ([National AI Centre](https://www.ai.gov.au/news-and-insights/blog/ai-adoption-insights-december-2025-february-2026)). This isn't because farmers avoid technology: **72%** already use agtech such as farm management software, electronic ID tags and satellite tools ([Roy Morgan, 2024](https://www.roymorgan.com/findings/9584-roy-morgan-farmer-agribusiness-survey-agtech-june-2024)).

Not only are these farms facing a severe ageing problem among owners and workers, but skilled farm labour is also in short supply. **Ageing:** Agriculture has the oldest owner-operators of any industry, and their median age rose from 51 to 56 between 2001 and 2016 ([ABARES, Agricultural workforce trends](https://www.agriculture.gov.au/abares/research-topics/agricultural-workforce/australian-agricultural-workforce-trends)). Livestock farmers now have a median age of **62**, compared with 39 across all occupations, and 34.6% are 65 or older ([Jobs and Skills Australia, Livestock Farmers](https://www.jobsandskills.gov.au/data/occupation-and-industry-profiles/occupations/1213-livestock-farmers)). The workforce behind them is shrinking: industry employment fell by 7,900 (–2.6%) in the year to February 2026, and the median worker is 51 ([Jobs and Skills Australia, Agriculture, Forestry and Fishing](https://www.jobsandskills.gov.au/data/occupation-and-industry-profiles/industries/agriculture-forestry-and-fishing)). **Skills:** 57% of horticulture farms had difficulty recruiting in 2021–22 ([ABARES, Labour use 2021–22](https://www.agriculture.gov.au/abares/research-topics/agricultural-workforce/labour-use/2022)), and 34% still did in 2022–23 ([ABARES via ARR News](https://arr.news/2024/11/26/farmers-finding-solutions-to-ease-labour-shortages-abares/)). Only about 37% of livestock farm workers hold a Certificate III or higher ([Jobs and Skills Australia, Livestock Farm Workers](https://www.jobsandskills.gov.au/data/occupation-and-industry-profiles/occupations/8415-livestock-farm-workers)). Farms also keep taking on workers with no farm-specific knowledge. 64% of contract horticulture workers now come through labour hire ([ABARES via ARR News](https://arr.news/2024/11/26/farmers-finding-solutions-to-ease-labour-shortages-abares/)), and the PALM scheme, a major source of seasonal farm labour, had 28,535 workers in December 2025 ([PALM scheme data](https://www.palmscheme.gov.au/palm-scheme-data)).

The main barriers to adoption in this industry are **price and perceived relevance**. Cost is the top barrier to new farm technology (66%), 23% of farmers see no need for it, and 19% see little benefit ([Roy Morgan, 2024](https://www.roymorgan.com/findings/9584-roy-morgan-farmer-agribusiness-survey-agtech-june-2024)). Across all SMEs, 54% of businesses not using AI say it isn't relevant to them ([National AI Centre](https://www.ai.gov.au/news-and-insights/blog/ai-adoption-insights-december-2025-february-2026)). Lack of knowledge (37%) and poor connectivity (36%) also rank highly ([Roy Morgan, 2024](https://www.roymorgan.com/findings/9584-roy-morgan-farmer-agribusiness-survey-agtech-june-2024)). Trusted advice is hard to reach, too. Farmers rely most on other farmers, farm consultants and input suppliers. Only 27% trust government agriculture departments and 19% trust banks ([QARS 2023–24 via Farm Online](https://www.farmonline.com.au/story/8719884/qars-survey-farmers-trust-ag-news-not-banks-or-social-media/)).

**Real Estate Management is also an area of opportunity given the wide array of workers.** Property managers coordinate landlords, tenants, tradespeople and strata. They help 1 in 3 Australians into homes ([MRI Software, 2024](https://www.mrisoftware.com/nz/news/innovation-and-focus-on-workplace-culture-drive-the-future-of-property-management/)) and manage about 90% of investment properties ([REIQ](https://www.reiq.com/resources/media-releases/rental-crisis-weighs-heavily-on-property-managers)). The rental, hiring and real estate services industry employs 235,200 people, 157,000 of them in real estate services ([Jobs and Skills Australia, Rental, Hiring and Real Estate Services](https://www.jobsandskills.gov.au/data/occupation-and-industry-profiles/industries/rental-hiring-and-real-estate-services)). **The workforce is under strain.** 29% of property managers intend to leave the profession, up from 13% in 2018. 66% say their workload is busy or far too busy, and 47% work more than 41 hours a week ([Real Estate Business, MRI Voice of the Property Manager 2024](https://www.realestatebusiness.com.au/industry/28712-property-manager-exodus-at-all-time-high-but-is-the-industry-turning-a-corner)). 54% report mental health struggles ([MRI Software, 2024](https://www.mrisoftware.com/nz/news/innovation-and-focus-on-workplace-culture-drive-the-future-of-property-management/)). **Unlike agriculture, the gap here is in how deeply AI is used, not whether it's used at all.** 11% of rental, hiring and real estate businesses use AI, close to the 12% national rate ([ABS, Characteristics of Australian Business 2024–25](https://www.abs.gov.au/statistics/industry/technology-and-innovation/characteristics-australian-business/latest-release)). 55% of property organisations use AI, but only 18% say their AI capability is "stable and growing", and internal skills are the top barrier (28%) ([Real Estate Business, Yardi & Property Council 2026](https://www.realestatebusiness.com.au/tech/31513-ai-adoption-accelerates-but-confidence-lags-in-property-sector)). Property managers are open to it: 72% see AI as a significant opportunity to improve productivity and work-life balance ([MRI Software, 2024](https://www.mrisoftware.com/nz/news/innovation-and-focus-on-workplace-culture-drive-the-future-of-property-management/)).

**Currently, the sector of choice is Real Estate Management **
